## the demo

Each frame is a grid of brightnesses. Turning one into text is picking a
character per cell — the **ramp** is that lookup, ordered from empty to solid,
and **contrast** is the curve applied before it.

<Demo />

Nothing here is a picture of the output. Move a control and the frame is rebuilt
from the grid, which is why every one of them is instant: they are all lookups
on numbers that already exist.

There is one control this panel does not have. **Density** decides how many
cells a frame is cut into, which is a property of the bake rather than the
reading — and this sheet was cut at 84 columns long before it reached the page.
There is nothing finer left in it to resolve, so the control is absent instead
of present and inert.

## the playground

Give it a source and the bake is yours to run. Same controls, plus the one the
demo cannot honour. One button, then the result — the stages are described
below rather than performed.

<Playground />

The file never leaves the tab. It becomes an object URL, gets decoded by a
`<video>` element, and every frame after that is read out of a canvas in your
own browser — there is no server in this, which is also why it is your machine
that does the work.

Drag the divider to compare. Both sides are driven by one clock: the ASCII
advances a frame and then seeks the video to match, rather than letting two
timers run and hoping they agree. A compare slider showing two different
instants is worse than no compare slider.

## how it works

Frames come out by **seeking**, never by playing — a clip played back in real
time gives you whatever frames the decoder felt like handing over, and seeking
to fixed offsets gives you the same 96 every run.

The subject is found by **occupancy**, not by a union of per-frame boxes. Union
boxes grow to fit the whole journey, so anything that moves ends up framed by
where it has been. Counting how many frames each column and row is actually lit
in, and cutting where that falls off, frames the thing instead.

Cells are averaged and normalised against **one global peak** across the whole
clip. Per-frame normalisation makes a dark frame as bright as a light one, and
the flicker that produces reads as a rendering fault.

Every one of those is a judgement call that looks fine in aggregate and wrong on
a specific clip, which is why the skill stops and asks before it writes a sheet.
The playground above does not — it has one clip and you can see the answer.

The skill ships its measurements too — including the frame rate that judders on
every frame by construction, and the re-centring that made motion three times
less stable than leaving it alone.
